Remote work changed where people sit during the day. Cities were built on the assumption that the answer was downtown, and the assumption was load-bearing.

The first-order effect is not complicated. Fewer people commute, so fewer people buy coffee, lunch, dry cleaning and transit fares within walking distance of an office tower. What makes this hard to measure is that office leases run long. Vacancy appears in the data years after the behaviour that caused it, which means the office market of any given year is still absorbing decisions made several years earlier. Any argument about causes has to account for that lag, and most do not.

The second-order effects are the ones that reach a city budget, and they arrive through a specific mechanism. Commercial property is assessed at a value tied to rents, rents follow occupancy, and a falling assessment cuts the revenue that pays for the transit system those commuters have stopped using. The squeeze runs from both ends at once. The tax base thins while the service obligations stay where they were, at least until a council is willing to cut a bus route in public.

There is a competing account, and it should be tested before this argument goes any further. Some of the activity that left the central business district did not disappear. It moved to smaller places with cheaper space and the same broadband. If that is mostly what happened, then the national picture is redistribution rather than loss, and the policy question changes shape entirely: not how to rescue a downtown, but how to share out the cost of a move that has already happened. Telling the two apart needs employment figures by metropolitan area. Aggregate office vacancy cannot do it, because the same number is produced by a firm that closed and a firm that left.

What can be said without that data is narrower. Zoning written for single-use office districts fits the emerging pattern badly, and a floorplate designed for cubicles converts into flats at a cost that shows up in permit counts and in which conversions never start. That is a claim about buildings rather than about the future of cities, and it has the advantage of being checkable.
