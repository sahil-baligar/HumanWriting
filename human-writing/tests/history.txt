The February Revolution of 1917 in Russia, also known as the February Revolution of 1917, was a pivotal period that saw the overthrow of Tsar Nicholas II and his autocratic rule. This revolution was precipitated by a complex interplay of internal and external factors, each contributing to the eventual collapse of the Russian Empire. The causes of the February Revolution can be broadly divided into socio-economic causes, political causes, and external causes, each playing a significant role in the unfolding of the revolution.

**Socio-Economic Causes:**

One of the most profound causes of the February Revolution was the severe economic strain and social unrest that the Russian Empire was experiencing during World War I. The country's economy was heavily reliant on the war effort, which not only provided employment but also increased its dependency on foreign aid. However, this dependency came at a significant cost. The war led to the diversion of resources to the military, leaving civilian life in a state of crisis. The food shortage, which became particularly acute, coupled with the rising cost of living, caused widespread hardship among the populace. As a result, the standard of living for the lower and middle classes plummeted, leading to significant dissatisfaction and protest. Strikes and riots began to erupt, as workers demanded better wages and conditions. This economic turmoil exacerbated existing social tensions and laid the groundwork for political upheaval.

Additionally, the socio-economic situation exacerbated by the war also exposed deep-seated inequalities within Russian society. The Russian nobility, which comprised a significant proportion of the population, often viewed the common people with disdain and exploitation. This class divide was further entrenched during the war, as the nobility saw the peasantry, who bore the brunt of the economic strain, as a source of cheap labor. The disparity between the wealth of the aristocracy and the poverty of the masses fueled resentment and contributed to the revolutionary sentiment.

**Political Causes:**

The political climate of the Russian Empire was similarly fraught with instability and discontent. The Tsarist regime, known for its autocratic style, had long been criticized for its inability to address the pressing issues facing the nation. The political system, which relied heavily on the patronage system and the nobility’s influence, effectively marginalized the common people. The absence of true political participation and the Tsar's reliance on the nobility for support created a system where the majority had little voice or agency. This political disenfranchisement was further compounded by the introduction of the 1906 Constitution, which, while granting some civil liberties, did little to alleviate the Tsar's autocratic rule.

The most significant political cause was the growing discontent within the elite class, particularly the military and the bureaucracy, who felt that the Tsarist regime was unable to manage the challenges of the war. They began to question the leadership's competence and legitimacy, which ultimately led to a growing support for political change. The military's increasing dissatisfaction, coupled with the Tsar's inability to address the war's dire consequences, created a critical juncture. The Russian army was in dire straits, with the loss of soldiers, food shortages, and a lack of supplies creating a sense of failure and disapproval.
