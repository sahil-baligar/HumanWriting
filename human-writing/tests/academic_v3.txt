Remote work moved a large share of white-collar labour out of central business districts, and cities had built their tax bases, transit systems and service economies on the assumption that the labour would stay there.

The direct effect runs through the commute. When fewer people travel downtown each day, demand falls for the businesses that existed because of the commute: coffee shops, lunch counters, dry cleaners, parking, and the transit fares themselves. Behind those are the services that supplied the offices rather than the workers, including catering, cleaning, maintenance and building security. These jobs are concentrated geographically in a way the office jobs are not, so a dispersed workforce does not take them along. A software engineer who moves to a smaller city keeps their salary. The person who cleaned their floor does not follow them [source needed for employment figures].

The fiscal mechanism is more specific than a general decline. Commercial property is assessed on a value derived from rents, and rents follow occupancy, so sustained vacancy lowers assessments and with them the property-tax revenue that funds transit and street maintenance. Sales-tax receipts fall at the same time and for the same reason, since the foot traffic that generated them has gone. The two revenue streams therefore move together and in the same direction, which is what makes the problem hard: a city cannot offset one with the other. Service obligations, meanwhile, do not fall in step. The bus route still runs, at lower farebox recovery, until a council votes in public to cut it.

The activity did not simply disappear. Firms and workers dispersed to smaller cities and towns with cheaper space and adequate broadband, which has produced a more polycentric pattern in which peripheral centres compete with traditional cores for the same investment. That dispersal favours work deliverable over a network, such as software, data analysis and marketing, and it raises the value of broadband access and digital skills in places that previously had neither. Municipal broadband programmes and workforce training are the policy responses that follow from this, and they are responses to the redistribution rather than to the decline.

Which of the two stories dominates is the question the argument turns on, and the answer determines what policy is for. If the loss is real, the task is to rescue a downtown. If it is redistribution, the task is to share out the cost of a move that has already happened. Zoning is where the two meet in practice: ordinances written for single-use office districts fit either outcome badly, and converting a floorplate designed for open-plan offices into housing is expensive enough that permit counts understate demand for conversion.
